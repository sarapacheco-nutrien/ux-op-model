# UX Design Thinking

**Owner:** Sara Pacheco — propose additions or changes via Slack or [sara.pacheco@nutrien.com](mailto:sara.pacheco@nutrien.com)

Foundational guidance for reasoning about Nutrien Digital products — who our users are, the conditions they work in, and how to think about design decisions on their behalf. Pull this in when making experience decisions, reviewing ideas, or generating UX recommendations.

## User Context

Nutrien Digital supports users across a wide variety of roles, including:

- Growers / Farmers
- Crop Consultants
- Agronomists
- Retail Agriculture Employees
- Operations Staff
- Internal Business Users

Users vary significantly in technical expertise, work environment, regional practices, and device preferences. Do not assume users are technology experts.

Prioritize: simplicity, clarity, trust, efficiency, accessibility, and error prevention.

---

## Agriculture Context

Agriculture is different from many industries. Users may interact with products in an office, at a retail location, in a vehicle, in a field, outdoors in bright sunlight, during busy seasonal periods, with inconsistent connectivity, and while multitasking.

Do not assume ideal usage conditions. Always account for:

- Environmental conditions
- Connectivity limitations
- Seasonal pressures
- Regional differences
- Operational complexity
- Real-world interruptions

## Guidance for AI Assistants

When supporting product decisions:

- Think about users before features.
- Think about workflows before screens.
- Think about outcomes before requirements.
- Understand the user's goal.
- Consider the user's environment.
- Reduce unnecessary complexity.
- Minimize cognitive load.
- Make important information easy to find.
- Use language familiar to the user.
- Support both new and experienced users.
- Consider accessibility from the beginning.
- Consider operational impacts.
- Consider error prevention.
- Challenge assumptions respectfully.
- Highlight risks and tradeoffs.
- Recommend validation when uncertainty exists.

When uncertainty exists:

- State what is known.
- State what is assumed.
- State what is unknown.
- Identify risks created by uncertainty.
- Recommend validation when confidence is low.
- Ask clarifying questions when additional information would materially improve the recommendation.
- If assumptions are required, clearly label them and proceed rather than stopping entirely.

When uncertain, prefer raising thoughtful questions over making unsupported assumptions.

## Usability Heuristics

Use these checks when reviewing ideas or generating recommendations.

### Visibility of System Status
Will users understand what is happening? Is feedback timely and clear?

### Match to Real-World Language
Does terminology match how users actually talk? Would a grower or employee understand it?

### User Control
Can users recover from mistakes? Can they undo or revise actions?

### Consistency
Does this follow existing patterns and expectations?

### Error Prevention
Can mistakes be prevented before they occur?

### Recognition Over Recall
Is information visible when needed? Does the user have to remember too much?

### Efficiency
Does it support both occasional and frequent users?

### Simplicity
Is unnecessary information removed? Can the workflow be streamlined?

### Error Recovery
Are errors understandable and actionable?

### Support
Can users get help if they need it?

## Operational Reality Check

Before finalizing a recommendation, always ask:

### 1. Where will the user be?
Could this be used indoors, outdoors, in a vehicle, in a branch, or in a field?

### 2. Have environmental conditions been considered?
Examples: bright sunlight, poor connectivity, interruptions, noise, equipment usage, mobile-only workflows.

### 3. Could this vary by region?
Consider terminology, crops, seasonal timing, business practices, and regulatory differences.

### 4. What happens if the user makes a mistake?
Consider financial impact, operational impact, customer impact, rework required, and loss of trust.

### 5. Is this usable during peak season?
Would this still work when users are busy, rushed, multitasking, or working under time-sensitive conditions?

## Designing for All Users

Design for a broad range of abilities and technology comfort levels. Consider readability, vision differences, color contrast, screen size constraints, touch target size, cognitive load, keyboard accessibility, and screen reader compatibility.

Many agriculture users may have decades of industry expertise but varying levels of comfort with digital tools. Do not equate domain expertise with technical expertise.

Note: technical accessibility requirements (touch target sizes, contrast ratios, keyboard order, WCAG AA pass/fail criteria) are defined in the Rise implementation guides.
